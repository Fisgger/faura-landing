Our Story

How Faura actually got built — including the parts that almost stopped it.

Faura started as a simple idea: a short-video app where people give aura instead of likes. What follows isn't the highlight reel. It's the version with the math that almost killed the plan, the tool that disappeared overnight, and the wall that changed the entire build strategy.

The Math Before the Mission

Before a single screen was designed, the question was whether a short-video app — built by one person, with AI handling the engineering — could realistically become a real business. The research wasn't encouraging: independent data from RevenueCat shows that 81% of apps never reach $1,000 a month in revenue within two years. That number reset the goal. It wasn't enough to make something that looked good; it had to survive that statistic.

The early research looked at apps that did — Cal AI, Pieter Levels' Photo AI, HabitKit — small teams, real revenue, no venture funding behind them. The plan for Faura targeted the U.S. market from day one, where ad rates run several times higher than in Latin America. A deliberate choice, not a default.

A Growth Loop That Didn't Survive Its Own Math

The first growth plan looked elegant on paper: pay for users through Meta Ads, let those users generate ad revenue inside the app, and reinvest that revenue into acquiring more users — a loop that funds itself.

Then came the real numbers. Acquiring a single user through Meta Ads costs somewhere between $2.50 and $5. That same user, watching ads inside the app, generates roughly $0.02 to $0.05 across their entire lifetime in the app — a cost 50 to 250 times higher than the return. The plan was scrapped on the spot, before a single line of code existed, because the numbers said so, not a hunch.

Designing for Restraint

The visual identity came next: a dark violet palette that avoids pure black, Instrument Serif paired with Inter, and one rule that governed every decision after it — restraint over spectacle. Nothing on Faura celebrates loudly.

That rule got tested almost immediately by its own most important element: the aura button. It started as a simple icon, then went through a comic-book-inspired version modeled on Into the Spider-Verse — vivid, kinetic, everything the rest of the app was built not to be. The resolution was a compromise that held: rich, textured motion for the instant of the tap, and calm for every second before and after it.

The Tool Everyone Assumes Exists

Months into building, a routine feature — trimming and editing video, the way TikTok does — ran into an unexpected wall. FFmpeg, the library nearly every mobile app in the world relies on for video processing, had quietly stopped being maintained in 2025, its binaries pulled from official repositories. It's the kind of dependency nobody double-checks, because everyone assumes it's just there.

The technical plan had to be rebuilt in real time — Skia took over live filters, and a different library, react-native-video-trim, handled real trimming months later.

An App Full of Ghosts

For weeks, Faura worked perfectly. Comments, saves, everything — functional and stable. What wasn't visible was that every session ran on a silent, anonymous login created automatically in the background. There was no real authentication system yet, and nothing about the app's behavior gave that away.

It surfaced only once mandatory onboarding was switched on for real — the moment it became clear the app had been quietly running without a single real user behind it.

The Wall Named Apple

The next obstacle had nothing to do with code. Testing the app on a physical iPhone, without owning a Mac, turned out to require an Apple Developer account — a mandatory $99 a year, with no free path around that specific combination.

Rather than pay for access to a platform the app wasn't ready for yet, the decision was to install Android Studio and keep building against an Android emulator, leaving Apple for a later stage of the project.

None of this is finished. Faura is still being built, one screen and one real bug at a time. Google Play itself won't let it reach the public without 12 real testers active for 14 straight days first — so the waitlist isn't a formality, it's the actual next step.
